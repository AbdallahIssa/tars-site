Where's the Pain? How TARS Can Take It Away and Make Work Easier

Most engineering time is not lost on hard problems. It is lost on finding things that someone, somewhere in the company, already knows.

Ask any senior engineer how their week went and you will rarely hear "I solved three hard problems." You will hear about the ticket that took two days because the relevant spec was in a folder nobody remembered. About explaining the same subsystem to the third new colleague this quarter. About setting up the test bench, again, to reproduce a bug that turned out to be a known issue from last year.

That is the pain. Not the difficult work, but everything wrapped around it.

Where the hours actually go

When we looked closely at how an investigation unfolds in a software team, the same pattern kept showing up:

  • Searching. The answer exists, but it is spread across code, requirements, test reports, tool manuals and old tickets, each in a different system with a different search box.
  • Reproducing. Before anyone can fix a problem, someone has to make it happen again, by hand, with the right tools in the right state.
  • Re-explaining. Knowledge lives in a few heads. Every question that reaches those people is a context switch away from their own work.
  • Writing it up. After the real work is done, the findings still need to become a clear ticket comment that the next person can trust.

None of these steps needs a senior engineer's judgement. All of them end up on a senior engineer's desk.

A teammate that has already read everything

TARS starts by learning. You point it at your repositories, documents, ticket system and tool manuals, and it builds a Knowledge Cave on your own machines. Adding a source takes minutes, and when files change, TARS reads only what changed.

From then on, the searching step mostly disappears. You can ask TARS a plain question, such as "what does module X.c do, and how does it affect stack Y?", and get an answer that points to the exact files and lines it came from. If the answer is not in your material, TARS says so instead of guessing.

The goal is simple: the knowledge your company already paid for should be available to everyone, instantly, without leaving the building.

From ticket to evidence

For real issues, you hand TARS the ticket. It reads it, collects the facts the ticket mentions, and works through the problem step by step while you watch. When it needs to use one of your tools, it uses capabilities it learned once and that your team reviewed. When an action would change something, it asks first.

What comes back is not a chat transcript. It is a finished analysis: what happened, how it was reproduced, the likely root cause, and next steps, with every claim linked to the evidence behind it. You can check the work in seconds, and post it to the ticket with one click, or keep it as a file if you prefer.

What changes for the team

The senior engineer stops being the search engine for the department. New colleagues ask TARS first and arrive at the senior's desk with better questions. Reproductions become repeatable scripts instead of one-off afternoons. And the write-up, the part everyone postpones, is ready when the investigation ends.

That is what we mean when we say TARS gives a senior engineer a whole team. Not a replacement for their judgement, but the legwork around it, done carefully, with receipts.

Why it has to stay inside

All of this only works if a company can hand TARS everything it knows. That is why TARS runs on your own hardware and does not need a cloud account. Your code, tickets and answers never leave your network. Privacy is not a feature we added at the end; it is the condition that makes the rest possible.
